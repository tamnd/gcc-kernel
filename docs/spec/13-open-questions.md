# Open questions

Each question has a proposed answer, the evidence that settles it, and the milestone by which it must be settled. None of them blocks G0.

**1. Is W(K, P) really an interval?** The frontier search (09.3) assumes it and samples the interior to check. If holes turn out to be common rather than rare (more than about 5% of rows), the search saves little and a dense sweep of tier 1 is cheaper to reason about. Proposed: keep the search, measure the hole rate on the dense stripes at G1 and G2, and switch tier 1 to dense if it is above 5%. Settled by G2.

**2. What counts as "runs correctly" for a museum kernel?** 2.6 and later have the smoke suite and, from 5.5, KUnit. A 1.2 kernel has fork, exec and a pipe. That is a much weaker claim, and the matrix prints the same `W` for both. Proposed: the published matrix marks museum cells with the suite they ran, and the heat map uses `W` only where the full smoke suite ran, `R` otherwise. Settled at G3.

Settled at G3, with one change. A museum cell keeps its verdict in `matrix/matrix.json`, so `works` there still means L8 of the ladder, and a reader of the JSON tells the suites apart by the kernel, since every kernel before 2.6 runs the museum suite of 07.5 and every later one runs the full suite. Showing those cells as `runs` would hide the one thing they do tell, that the kernel boots, runs PID 1 and passes fork, exec, a pipe, a file on the root file system, sync and uname, so the heat maps give them a square of their own instead: a green circle in the Markdown reports and the README, and a lighter green on the site. A green square is kept for the full suite. The four museum cells that reach L8 on i386 with their era GCC, 1.2.13, 2.0.40, 2.2.26 and 2.4.37.11, are the first to carry it.

**3. What does a cell actually cost?** Document 09's estimates are made before anything has run. G0 measures builds of modern kernels, G1 boots and KUnit, G3 the legacy builds. If the tier 1 sweep comes to more than about twice the estimate, tier 2 moves to Current and the longterm stripe only. Settled by G1 for modern kernels, G3 for the rest.

Measured at G1, for modern kernels. The cells are 7.2.8 cells on server2, a 6 core VPS that other jobs were loading to an average of 15 to 68 the whole time, so the build times are upper bounds. Under that load the x86_64 `defconfig+gk` build took 2564 seconds with GCC 16.2.0 and 3979 with 14.2.0, against 722 to 765 when the machine was quiet at G0. One boot to L6 under TCG takes 13 to 15 seconds, so three boots cost under a minute. KUnit is the expensive part: the three runs of L7 took 958 seconds, about 320 each, which matches the 328 seconds of 06.7. On arm64 a `tinyconfig+gk` cell built in 589 seconds, booted in 6 or 7, and ran its 63 suites three times in 305. The arm64 `defconfig+gk` build did not finish inside its 90 minute budget under that load. On a quiet 6 core machine a modern `defconfig+gk` cell to L8 therefore costs about half an hour: 12 or 13 minutes of build, 16 of KUnit and 2 of everything else. That is about five times the 9.2 estimate, well over the factor of two this question set as the limit, so tier 2 moves to the Current set and the longterm stripe only. KUnit is about half of a cell, and running it once and only repeating it when the run differs from the era cell's would take a cell back to about 20 minutes. That is proposed for G2, and the three run rule for boots stays. Settled for modern kernels. The older eras are still G3's.

Measured at G3 for the museum, on the same server2 with three jobs a cell and a load average of 50 to 70 the whole time, so again these are upper bounds. A 1.2.13 cell to L8 took 71 to 116 seconds, of which the build was 23 to 40 and each of the three boots 11 to 17. 2.0.40 took 121 to 149 seconds with a build of 46 to 69. 2.2.26 took 131 to 567, and the slowest of those spent 236 seconds in L1 alone, unpacking the tree while the disk was busy, so its build of 63 to 121 seconds is the fairer number. 2.4.37.11 took 230 to 732 seconds with a build of 155 to 578 and boots of 10 to 30 seconds each. A cell that fails at L2 or L3 costs 10 to 80 seconds, and most columns of a museum row are those, so the dense row of 1.0 with its 38 columns took about 35 minutes in all. Against the 9.2 table the boots are on the estimate and the builds are within two to three times it under that load, which is inside the factor of two on a quiet machine. The museum is about 25 kernels on one platform, so even run dense it is under a day of one job. Nothing in tier 1 or tier 2 changes for it. Settled for the museum. The first 2.6 rows, run again after the races of #99 and #102 on the same loaded server2 with three jobs a cell, give the rest. A 2.6.0 to 2.6.2 cell that builds spends 270 to 425 seconds in the build on i386 or x86_64. Most columns of those rows stop earlier: a GCC from 4.0 to 4.4 is refused by the accept probe in 7 to 16 seconds, and one that fails in the build tree costs 11 to 48 seconds. A boot that panics costs 2 to 4 seconds, and one that hangs costs the whole 300 second timeout, so a 3.2.3 cell of 2.6.0 that hangs took 728 seconds in all. At the other end of the line, a 2.6.32 x86_64 cell to L8 with 4.3.5 took 767 seconds, 706 of them the build, with boots of 15 or 16 seconds. A dense 2.6 row of about 50 columns on both platforms comes to about 20 minutes, and the 2.6 history from 2.6.0 to 2.6.39 to about 13 hours of one job. The builds are within the factor of two of the 9.2 table under that load, as in the museum, so nothing in tier 1 or tier 2 changes. Settled for the legacy history.

**4. Can Linus's `-mstring-insns` GCC be found or rebuilt?** 0.01 to 0.12 need it. The oldlinux archives hold GCC 1.40 binaries for Linux 0.1x, which may be the patched build, and the patch itself may be recoverable from them. Proposed: the 0.x experiment of 07.7, time-boxed to a week. A patched GCC 1.40 rebuilt from a recovered patch would violate "unmodified toolchains" (02.5), so it would produce an ungraded report, not cells. Settled at G3.

Settled at G3: it was found, and nothing had to be rebuilt. The oldlinux archive's `gccbin.tar.Z` holds Linus's GCC 1.40 binaries of 22 September 1991, and their `gcc-cc1` takes `-mstring-insns`. With them, pristine 0.11 and 0.12 from kernel.org build inside the oldlinux Linux 0.11 development system and boot to a shell, under Bochs. The gcc 1.40 that system ships is a 2004 rebuild that refuses the flag, which is the `private-gcc-flag` failure the question expected. 0.01 does not build there, because its Makefile wants the Minix assembler and Minix's `ls`. QEMU runs none of these kernels, since its floppy and IDE models stop their drivers. The binaries are used as they were released, so no toolchain was modified, but the runs were done by hand and are not cells. [reports/museum-0x.md](../../reports/museum-0x.md) has the whole result.

**5. How far back can the forge build unpatched?** Building GCC 2.x in an i386 Debian container of its time should work, since that is how Debian built it. GCC 1.x in an emulated Linux 0.12 might not. Anything that needs a patch to build is **unbuildable** by rule, and the matrix loses those columns. Proposed: accept the loss and document it. Revisit only if a whole era has no buildable column. Settled at G3.

The 1.x part is settled at G3 without a forge: GCC 1.x is not rebuilt at all, because the original 1991 binaries exist and work (question 4). For 2.x, 2.5.8 builds unpatched in the slink forge. Its `gcc.c` and `cccp.c` declare `sys_errlist` without the `const` that glibc gives it, unless `bsd4_4` is defined, and in those two files `bsd4_4` decides nothing else, so the forge passes `-Dbsd4_4` on the make command line and the sources stay as released. 2.7.2.3 and later build as they are.

**6. Distribution columns beyond Debian and Ubuntu.** Fedora, Arch and SUSE ship GCC with their own defaults, and Arch ships new GCC majors within weeks, so Arch users hit new breakages first. Proposed: no columns for them until a finding shows a kernel break specific to that distribution's GCC that the upstream column does not show. Settled at G5.

At G5 the GCC packages of the three were checked on 5 October 2026, all three at 16.2.1, by asking each compiler what it defines and enables with no flags given. Fedora's `gcc` turns nothing on: no PIE, no stack protector, no `-fcf-protection`, no stack clash protection. Its hardening lives in `redhat-rpm-config`, which only a package build reads. openSUSE Tumbleweed's `gcc` is the same, and default PIE comes from a separate `gcc-PIE` package that adds it through a specs file. Arch's `gcc` is configured with `--enable-default-pie` and `--enable-default-ssp`, so it builds PIE with `-fstack-protector-strong` unless told otherwise. Each of those defaults is already one the Debian and Ubuntu columns carry, and Ubuntu's set is a superset of Arch's, so a kernel break caused by an Arch default shows up in an Ubuntu column first. Arch shipping a new major early is covered by the upstream column, which runs from the day of the release. Settled: no Fedora, Arch or SUSE columns. The question opens again if a finding shows a break that only one of those compilers produces.

**7. Should rucc-kernel use gcc-kernel's host images and toolchain bundles?** The kernel plan's era containers and gcc-kernel's hosts overlap. Sharing them saves rucc-kernel a forge and makes both repositories use the same period GCC byte for byte. It also makes rucc-kernel depend on a registry it does not own. Proposed: rucc-kernel pins gcc-kernel images and bundles by digest in its `toolchains.toml`, as data, and keeps its own Dockerfiles as a fallback. The decision is rucc-kernel's. Settled when rucc-kernel reaches its museum milestones (K10, K11).

**8. KVM or TCG for x86 boots?** KVM is much faster and the budget depends on it (09.2), but it exposes the machine's CPU to old kernels. Proposed: KVM from 2.6.25 on after the G1 comparison of 06.6, TCG before. If the comparison shows any verdict difference on the era diagonal, TCG everywhere and accept the cost. Settled at G1.

At G1 none of the three machines this repository runs on has `/dev/kvm`, so the comparison of 06.6 could not be run. Every boot is TCG, which is the reference anyway, and the budgets in `platforms.toml` assume it. The measured TCG cost (question 3) is small for boots and large for KUnit, so KVM would mostly save KUnit time. Settled for G1 as TCG everywhere. The comparison runs on the first machine with KVM that joins, and until it has run, no cell is booted under KVM.

**9. `CONFIG_WERROR` and arch `-Werror`.** The matrix builds with `CONFIG_WERROR=n`, but several architectures and directories have their own `-Werror` (sparc, powerpc's `PPC_WERROR`, alpha, mips, some drivers, objtool). A new warning therefore fails some platforms and not others with the same GCC. That is a real finding, and the L3w twin shows it, but it could also make tier 3 look worse than it is. Proposed: report it as is, with the class `new-warning-werror`, and never turn off an architecture's own `-Werror` (that would change the kernel, which 02.5 forbids). Settled by G4.

Settled at G4 as proposed. The fragments turn off `CONFIG_WERROR` and nothing else of the kind, so `PPC_WERROR`, the per-directory `-Werror` lines and objtool's own stay as the kernel has them, and a platform fragment never touches them either. A warning they make fatal is a `fails` at L3 with a class of kind `new-warning-werror`: a named class where the catalog knows the warning, and `arch-werror` for the rest, which sits last in `signatures.toml` so that a named class always wins. The heat maps show those cells like any other failure. The L3w twin, which builds with `KCFLAGS=-Wno-error`, shows that such a cell failed on a warning and not on an error, which is how tier 3 is read without looking worse than it is.

**10. `allmodconfig` on the Current set.** It exercises far more code than `defconfig`, and it is where most new-GCC breakages first show. It is also about ten times as expensive. Proposed: `allmodconfig` build-only on the Current set, the GCC 16 column and the newest two last points, x86_64 and arm64 only. Measured at G1 with 7.2.8 on x86_64 and gcc-16.2.0, on a shared 6 core machine with 11.7 GB: 26,633 compile units in 3 h 38 min, where `defconfig+gk` takes 12 to 43 min on the same machine depending on its load, so close to the ten times guessed. The build then did not finish. objtool on `vmlinux.o` grew past 3.5 GB while the machine was full, and the OOM killer stopped it. A killed build is now no verdict at all, instead of a failure charged to the GCC. Settled: `allmodconfig` stays build-only and out of the stripe. It runs on demand, on x86_64 and arm64, for the newest Current kernel and the GCC 16 column, and only on a host that has several GB free when the link starts.

**11. Clang.** The question the user asked is about GCC, and rucc aims at GCC compatibility. A clang axis would reuse everything here: host environments, the ladder, the boot rig and the catalog. Proposed: out of scope. The design keeps `flavor` open, so a `clang` flavor column could be added later without changing the model. Not scheduled.

**12. Rust.** `CONFIG_RUST=n` everywhere. Kernel Rust needs its own rustc and bindgen versions, which is a third axis. Proposed: out of scope, like clang. Not scheduled.

**13. Should every log be kept?** Document 09.9 keeps full `make` logs and `compile.jsonl` only for edge cells and holes, about 5 GB. That is enough to explain every edge, but a later question about an interior cell (for example, a new warning census over an old sweep) then needs a rebuild. Keeping everything is a few hundred GB per full sweep, compressed **(estimate, measured at G1)**. Proposed: keep everything for the Current set and the GCC 16 column, edges and holes elsewhere, and rebuild on demand, which identity makes exact. Settled at G2.

Measured at G2, on the 72 cells of server3's store, which are modern `defconfig+gk` cells that reached L4 to L8 and keep every log. They come to 2.3 GB, about 32 MB a cell, and 95% of it is `compile.jsonl`, 30 MB on average. Everything else is small: the compressed `make` log is 0.16 MB, the `.config` 0.15 MB, the three KUnit logs 0.9 MB together, and the three boot consoles 0.1 MB. `compile.jsonl` is JSON lines with the same paths and flags on every line, and zstd at level 19, as `gk store pack` uses, takes one from 32 MB to 0.5 MB. A modern cell to L8 is therefore about 1 MB packed, and a failing cell of the 2.6 rows, which stops at the probe or early in the build, keeps 150 to 350 KB of text before packing, a few tens of KB after. Even if every one of the 16,000 tier 1 cells and the 4,000 of the dense stripes kept 1 MB, a full sweep would be about 20 GB packed, not a few hundred. So every log of every cell is kept, and the edge cell rule of 9.9 is dropped. The one cost is on the machine that runs the sweep, where an unpacked modern cell is 32 MB: the working store is packed with `gk store pack` and cleared as a sweep goes, as server3's has been. Settled.
